Project Overview

A user reported that their Uniswap V3 swap had failed and gas was consumed, but no tokens were received. Using Etherscan and Tenderly, I performed a complete transaction post-mortem — identifying the exact revert reason, reconstructing pool state at execution time, ruling out alternative failure causes, and delivering a clear resolution with actionable guidance.

Investigation

User report: Uniswap swap failed, gas consumed, no tokens received. Transaction hash provided.

Steps Taken

  • Opened the transaction hash on Etherscan. Confirmed status: Failed. Retrieved the on-chain revert reason: Too little received — the V3-specific error confirming a slippage failure, not a deadline or gas exhaustion revert.
  • Checked gas used vs gas limit. The transaction consumed the majority of its gas budget before reverting, confirming the revert occurred during execution (mid-swap, after entering the pool but before final settlement). This distinction is important: a pre-flight revert would consume minimal gas and point to a different class of issue.
  • Decoded the swap input data: amountIn, amountOutMinimum, sqrtPriceLimitX96, deadline, and the token path. Confirmed the deadline parameter was valid and had not expired at the time of execution — ruling out a deadline revert as a contributing cause.
  • Used Tenderly to replay the transaction at the exact block it was mined. Inspected the full execution trace to confirm the point of failure: the router's final check comparing actual output to amountOutMinimum failed, triggering the revert.
  • Examined the Uniswap V3 pool state at that block — tick data, liquidity depth, and price impact for the trade size. Price had moved approximately 1.8% between quote and execution, exceeding the user's configured 0.5% slippage tolerance.
  • Reviewed the surrounding blocks and mempool context to assess MEV exposure. Found no evidence of a sandwich attack targeting this transaction — the pool price movement was consistent with broader market activity across multiple pools in the same timeframe, not isolated manipulation.
  • Compared the failed transaction parameters against a successful swap in the same pool shortly after, confirming that a higher slippage tolerance or a smaller trade size would have succeeded under the same conditions.

Root Cause

The transaction reverted with Too little received — the Uniswap V3 router's slippage guard triggered because the actual output amount fell below the user's amountOutMinimum threshold. Price moved approximately 1.8% between quote generation and block execution, exceeding the configured 0.5% tolerance.

This is a common and expected outcome when trading lower-liquidity or high-volatility tokens during active market conditions. From the user’s perspective, this can look like “gas burned for nothing”, but on-chain it is a correct enforcement of their own slippage guard. Gas was consumed because the revert occurred after execution began — not a bug, but a deliberate safety mechanism.

MEV/front-running was considered and ruled out based on pool state analysis and block context. The price movement was consistent with organic market activity.

Resolution & Outcome

  • Explained the revert mechanism in plain language: the transaction didn't fail due to an error — it failed because the protocol was protecting the user. Gas is consumed regardless once execution begins.
  • Provided specific guidance on slippage tolerance: recommended 1–2% for this token's typical volatility profile, with an explanation of the trade-off between protection and execution success rate.
  • Advised splitting larger trades into smaller tranches to reduce price impact, particularly for lower-liquidity pools.
  • Recommended enabling MEV protection (e.g., Flashbots Protect RPC) for future high-value swaps, even where MEV was not the issue here — proactive advice appropriate for this user's trade size.
  • Documented the investigation as a support runbook entry covering the two most common Uniswap revert patterns: Too little received (V3 slippage) and UniswapV2Router: INSUFFICIENT_OUTPUT_AMOUNT (V2 equivalent), including diagnostic steps, Tenderly replay instructions, and MEV assessment criteria.
  • Added this pattern to the internal troubleshooting library, reducing time-to-answer for similar “failed swap” tickets by providing a reusable diagnostic workflow.

Key Takeaways

"Failed transaction" reports in crypto support are rarely straightforward. The same symptom — gas consumed, no tokens received — can stem from slippage failure, an expired deadline, gas exhaustion, a contract-level revert, or MEV interference. Each has a different root cause, a different explanation, and different remediation advice. The ability to distinguish between them quickly and confidently using on-chain tooling is what separates effective crypto support from guesswork.

One pattern worth noting: users frequently interpret a "Failed" transaction as a platform error and expect compensation or a refund. Resolving this well requires both technical precision and clear communication — the investigation needs to be thorough enough to explain exactly what happened and why, not just reassure the user that nothing went wrong.
